> **Short answer:** Pick a money market account by three things in this order — the tier your balance actually lands in, the transaction limits your monthly habits will run into, and the fee schedule. The advertised APY is the last thing to compare, because it is usually the top-tier rate and most people never earn it.

Two money market accounts can advertise the same rate and pay you very different amounts. The difference is almost never the rate itself. It is which slice of your balance the rate applies to, whether you keep the minimum every single day of the cycle, and how many transfers you make before the bank starts charging per item.

This guide is about picking one. If you are still deciding whether a money market account is the right product at all, start with [what a money market account is and how it works](/SmartMoneyDaily/2026/06/02/what-is-a-money-market-account-and-how-does-it-work/) and come back here.

## Start With How You Will Use the Cash, Not the Headline APY

Before you open a comparison tab, write down three things about the money you plan to park:

1. **The balance you will actually hold on the worst day of the month.** Not the average, not the peak. Tier tests and fee waivers are often applied to the lowest balance in the statement cycle, so that number is what decides your rate.
2. **How many times cash leaves the account per month.** Count transfers to checking, bill payments, checks, and card swipes separately — banks count them separately too.
3. **How fast you need the money.** Same-day at a branch or ATM is a different requirement than an ACH transfer that settles in a couple of business days.

Those three answers eliminate most offers before you ever look at a rate. A 12-transfer-per-month saver and a 1-transfer-per-month saver should not be shopping the same accounts.

## Tier Tables: Finding the Rate That Actually Applies

Most money market accounts are tiered. The bank publishes a table of balance ranges, each with its own rate. What the table does not always say plainly is *which of two methods* it uses:

- **Whole-balance tiering.** Your entire balance earns the rate of the tier you land in. Cross into a higher tier and every dollar gets the better rate.
- **Split-rate (blended) tiering.** Each portion of your balance earns the rate of its own tier. The top rate applies only to the dollars above the top threshold.

The advertised "up to X% APY" is the top tier under either method. Under split-rate tiering, almost nobody earns it.

For example, suppose a bank publishes this table:

| Balance range | Rate on that portion |
|---|---|
| $0 – $9,999 | 0.50% |
| $10,000 – $49,999 | 3.00% |
| $50,000 and above | 3.25% |

Now put $12,000 in it.

- **Whole-balance method:** the full $12,000 earns 3.00%, roughly $360 in a year before compounding effects and tax.
- **Split-rate method:** $10,000 earns 0.50% ($50) and $2,000 earns 3.00% ($60). Total: $110. Your effective yield is $110 ÷ $12,000 = **0.92%** — against a headline of 3.25%.

Run the same table at $60,000 and split-rate tiering gives you $50 + $1,200 + $325 = $1,575, an effective 2.63%. Still not the headline. The gap only closes as your balance climbs far past the top threshold.

**What to do:** ask the bank in writing — chat transcript or email is fine — "Is the tier rate applied to my entire balance or only to the portion within each tier?" The Truth in Savings rule (12 CFR Part 1030) requires the account disclosure to explain how the rate is determined, so the answer exists in the paperwork even when the marketing page skips it.

## The Tier Cliff, and Why One Bad Day Costs a Month

Tiers have edges, and the edge is tested on a date you may not be watching. Read the disclosure for which balance the bank uses to set your tier: the **lowest daily balance**, the **average daily balance**, or the **balance on the last day of the cycle**.

Under a lowest-daily-balance rule, a single dip below a threshold — a rent payment that clears a day before your paycheck lands — can push the whole cycle down a tier. Under an average-daily-balance rule, the same dip barely registers. Same balance, same bank, different sentence in the disclosure, different money.

If your balance sits close to a threshold, either keep a buffer above it or choose an account whose tier test is average-based.

## Minimums That Bite: Opening, Ongoing, and Tier

Three different minimums hide under the same word, and an account can have all three:

| Minimum type | What it controls | What happens if you miss it |
|---|---|---|
| Opening deposit | Whether you can open the account at all | Application is declined or held |
| Ongoing balance | Whether the monthly maintenance fee is waived | Fee is charged, often monthly |
| Tier minimum | Which rate applies | You drop to a lower tier's rate |

They are frequently different numbers. An account can waive its monthly fee at one balance while paying its good rate only at a much higher one. Check whether the fee waiver can also be earned another way — a linked checking account, a recurring direct deposit, or a combined-balance test across accounts at the same institution are all common alternatives.

## Withdrawal and Transfer Limits After the Reg D Change

The old six-per-month ceiling on convenient transfers came from Regulation D. The [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/reservereq.htm) amended the rule in 2020 and removed that limit. What did not happen: banks were not required to change their own account terms, and many kept the limit anyway.

So the limit you face is now a *bank policy*, not a federal rule, and it varies. When you read the terms, get answers to four things:

- How many transfers or withdrawals per statement cycle are free?
- Which transaction types count — ACH out, online transfer, check, debit card, bill pay, wire?
- What is the per-item fee once you pass the limit?
- What happens if you exceed it repeatedly? Some institutions convert the account to a checking or standard savings product, which changes your rate entirely.

That last one matters more than the fee. Getting converted out of the product silently is a worse outcome than paying a few dollars.

## Match Your Monthly Habits to the Limits You Need

Use your actual transaction count from the last three statements, not an estimate.

| Your monthly cash activity | What the account must offer | What disqualifies it |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 transfers, savings only | Nothing special; optimize for rate and fee waiver | Nothing — you will never touch the limit |
| 3–6 transfers, sinking funds | A stated limit of 6+ and free ACH out | Per-item fees starting at the third transfer |
| Bill paying from the account | Check writing or bill pay, generous limit | Check-writing sold as an add-on with a per-check fee |
| Occasional cash needs | Debit card plus a surcharge-free ATM network | Card issued only on request, or ATM fees not reimbursed |
| Frequent, unpredictable movement | Consider a checking account paired with savings instead | Any account that converts you out after repeat overages |

## Check Writing, Debit Access, and ATM Networks

Check-writing and a debit card are what separate a money market account from a plain savings account — but "offers checks" covers a wide range. Confirm: whether checks are free or ordered at a cost, whether there is a per-check fee, whether the debit card comes automatically, and which ATM network you get. An account with a small in-network footprint and no out-of-network reimbursement quietly charges you every time you need cash away from home.

If none of these features matter to you, a money market account may be the wrong shape entirely. Compare against the checklist in [what to check before opening a high-yield savings account](/SmartMoneyDaily/2026/06/15/what-to-consider-when-opening-a-high-yield-savings-account/) before you commit.

## Insurance Check: Bank, Credit Union, or Fintech Partner

Deposit insurance is standard for money market accounts held at insured institutions — federal deposit insurance covers up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category, and the [NCUA](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage) provides parallel share insurance at federally insured credit unions. The part worth checking is *who actually holds the deposit*.

- **A chartered bank.** Confirm the institution in the FDIC's [BankFind](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/) directory. Note the brand name may differ from the charter name.
- **A credit union.** Verify it is federally insured; membership eligibility is a separate question.
- **A fintech app.** Many apps are not banks. They place your money with one or more partner banks. Find the partner bank list in the app's deposit agreement, confirm each one is insured, and understand that your coverage runs through those banks — including any balance you already hold at the same bank directly.

Confusing an app's branding with a charter is one of the more expensive mistakes available in personal finance. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/bank-accounts/) publishes plain-language material on how deposit accounts work if you want a neutral second reference.

## Using an MMA as a Bills Buffer or Sinking-Fund Hub

A money market account earns its place when the money needs to be both productive and reachable. Two jobs it does well:

**Bills buffer.** Hold one to two months of fixed bills in the account and pay large, irregular bills — insurance premiums, tuition, quarterly taxes — directly from it by check or transfer. The balance stays high enough to clear tier and waiver thresholds, and the checks avoid the transfer-limit problem entirely at institutions that count checks separately.

**Sinking-fund hub.** Keep several goals in one account and track them in a spreadsheet rather than opening a separate account per goal. One larger balance is more likely to clear a top tier than four small ones, and you only manage one set of minimums. Some banks offer named sub-accounts or "buckets" inside a single money market account, which does the same thing without the spreadsheet.

What it does not do well: money you will not touch for years. A term deposit will usually pay more for a commitment, and money you will touch weekly usually belongs in checking.

## Deal-Breakers Hidden in the Fee Schedule

The fee schedule is a separate document from the rate sheet, and it is where the account's real cost lives. Scan for these:

- **Monthly maintenance fee with a waiver you will not always meet.** A fee charged even a few months a year can exceed the interest advantage over a plain savings account at modest balances.
- **Excess transaction fee stacked per item.** Three overages in one cycle is three charges, not one.
- **Paper statement fee**, unless you switch to electronic delivery.
- **Dormancy or inactivity fee** on an account you intend to leave alone — a real risk for a sinking fund you fund once and forget.
- **Outgoing wire and expedited transfer fees**, which matter if this is where your emergency cash lives.
- **Stop-payment and check reorder fees**, if you plan to use the check-writing feature.

For a fuller walkthrough of converting each of these into an annual dollar figure, see [the fees that quietly eat your savings interest](/SmartMoneyDaily/2026/06/26/how-to-identify-and-avoid-common-fees-in-high-yield-options/).

## Choosing an MMA: Questions to Settle First

**How do I find the current going rate for money market accounts?**
The FDIC publishes national deposit rate data monthly. Look it up and use it as a baseline, then compare individual offers against it. Any single figure you read in an article was out of date the week it was published.

**Is a higher advertised APY always the better account?**
No. Compare the rate that applies to *your* balance under *that bank's* tiering method, then subtract fees you will realistically pay. An account with a lower headline and whole-balance tiering can beat a higher headline with split-rate tiering at the same deposit.

**Can I lose money in a money market account?**
The principal in an insured deposit account is protected up to the coverage limits. Fees can exceed interest at low balances, which is a loss in practical terms. Money market *funds* are a different product with no deposit insurance — see [how money market funds work and where the risk hides](/SmartMoneyDaily/2026/06/25/a-comprehensive-guide-to-finding-the-right-money-market-fund/).

**What happens if my balance falls below the minimum?**
Depending on the disclosure, one or more of: the monthly fee is charged, your rate drops to a lower tier, or both. Some accounts also stop paying interest entirely below a floor balance.

**Will my rate stay where it is?**
No. Money market rates are variable and the bank can reprice them. Set a calendar reminder to re-check yours quarterly — [why money market rates move and how fast yours follows](/SmartMoneyDaily/2026/06/07/how-changing-interest-rates-affect-your-money-market-account-returns/) covers how to spot a change on your statement.

## Narrowing It Down to One Account

Pull the last three statements of whatever account currently holds this money and write down two numbers: your lowest balance and your monthly transaction count. Take those to two candidate accounts, fill in the scorecard above, and confirm the tiering method in writing before you fund anything. That is a thirty-minute exercise that decides your yield for the next several years — considerably better use of the time than reading one more rate roundup.
