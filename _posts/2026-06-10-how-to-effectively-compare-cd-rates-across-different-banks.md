> **Short answer:** Compare offers on one line — same deposit, same number of days, APY instead of the nominal rate — and judge them by the dollars you would hold on the maturity date after penalties, fees, and tax. Banks quote different rates in the same week because each one is funding a different balance sheet, not because one is more generous than the other.

A CD rate is a price. The bank is borrowing your money for a fixed number of days and telling you what it will pay for the privilege. Once you read it that way, the comparison stops being a hunt for the biggest number on a rate table and becomes a short arithmetic exercise: which contract leaves the most money in your account on the day it matures.

## Why Two Banks Quote Very Different Rates the Same Week

Deposit pricing is set institution by institution, not handed down from a central table. A bank with a full loan pipeline and thin deposit growth will bid aggressively for 12-month money. A bank sitting on more deposits than it can lend has no reason to pay up, and its published CD rates will look unremarkable no matter what is happening in the wider market.

That is why a small credit union can beat a national bank on a 13-month special while trailing badly on every other term, and why the ranking of "best rates" reshuffles constantly. You are not looking at a market consensus. You are looking at dozens of separate funding decisions that happen to be printed on the same comparison page.

The practical consequence: never assume that the institution that won last time still wins. And never assume the headline rate is the whole offer — it is one field in a contract with a dozen fields, which is the subject of a separate walkthrough on [the nine terms to check before you sign a CD](/SmartMoneyDaily/2026/07/08/how-to-choose-the-best-certificate-of-deposit-for-your-savings-goals/).

## The Three Drivers Behind Any Quote

**Policy rates set the direction.** The Federal Reserve's target for the federal funds rate anchors what banks can earn on their own short-term cash, so deposit pricing generally drifts in the same direction the [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy.htm) moves. What no rule requires is that any particular share of a policy change reaches your account, or that it arrives on any particular schedule. Banks reprice new-account offers when it suits their funding plan. A CD you already own is unaffected either way — the rate is fixed by contract for the full term.

**The bank's own funding need sets the intensity.** Loan demand, deposit outflows, and seasonal cash swings decide whether an institution wants your money badly this quarter. Banks often want money for a specific window rather than in general, which is where oddly shaped specials come from: a 7-month, 11-month, or 13-month term exists because it lands where the bank has a gap.

**Cost of delivery sets the floor.** Branches, staff, and older systems cost money per dollar of deposits held. Institutions without that overhead — online-only banks and many credit unions — can pay more and still clear their margin. This is a structural difference, not a promotion, which is why the gap between branch-heavy and branch-light institutions tends to persist across rate cycles.

## What Kind of Rate You Are Actually Buying

"CD rate" covers several different contracts. Sort the offer into one of these before you compare anything:

| Rate type | What is guaranteed | When it can change | What to check first |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed | Rate and APY locked for the full term | Never during the term | What rate the renewal term uses |
| Bump-up | Fixed, plus the right to request the bank's current rate for the same term | Only when you ask, usually once | You are limited to that bank's own rate at that moment |
| Step-up | Increases already written into the disclosure schedule | On the dates named in the contract | The blended rate over the whole term, not the final step |
| Variable / index-linked | The formula, not the rate | On each repricing date in the contract | How the index is defined and how often it resets |
| Promotional special | Fixed, for an unusual term length | At maturity | The renewal term and its rate basis differ from the promo |
| Brokered | The coupon, if you hold to maturity | Market price moves daily; issuer may call it | Call provisions and what you would net by selling early |

A step-up CD advertised by its final step is the most common source of disappointment here. If the contract raises the rate in stages, the number that matters is the average across the whole term, not the peak you reach in the final months.

## APY, Not the Interest Rate

Two offers with the same nominal rate can pay different amounts, because compounding frequency differs. That is precisely the problem annual percentage yield was created to solve. Under the Truth in Savings Act and its implementing rule, [Regulation DD (12 CFR Part 1030)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/), depository institutions must disclose APY using a uniform formula, so APY is the only field that lets you line up offers from different institutions.

The relationship is:

**APY = (1 + r/n)ⁿ − 1**

where *r* is the nominal annual rate and *n* is the number of compounding periods per year.

For example, suppose a bank quotes a 4.00% nominal rate compounded daily. Then n = 365, and the APY works out to about 4.081%. The same 4.00% compounded annually is exactly 4.00% APY. On a $10,000 deposit held one year, that is roughly $408 versus $400 — small, but it is free money you get by reading one extra line.

Two rules follow. If an advertisement shows a rate without an APY, treat the offer as incomplete and find the disclosure before you fund anything. And if you want to see how frequency, balance, and time interact across account types, the mechanics are worked through in detail in the guide to [APY, compounding, and what your savings will really earn](/SmartMoneyDaily/2026/07/04/understanding-apy-what-it-means-for-your-savings-strategy/).

## Converting Odd Terms Back to an Annual Basis

Promotional maturities exist partly because they are hard to compare. An 11-month special and a 12-month standard CD do not cover the same amount of time, so their APYs are not directly comparable in dollars.

Handle it by choosing a common horizon and pricing the gap:

1. Work out the maturity value of the shorter CD.
2. Assume a rate you can actually get today for the leftover days — your savings account, not a hoped-for future CD.
3. Add that stub to the shorter CD's result and compare totals.

For example, suppose you have $10,000 and two offers: an 11-month special at 4.25% APY, and a 12-month CD at 4.10% APY.

- 11-month special: $10,000 × (1.0425)^(11/12) − $10,000 ≈ **$388.90**
- Park the proceeds for the remaining month at an assumed 3.50% APY: $10,388.90 × [(1.035)^(1/12) − 1] ≈ **$29.83**
- Twelve-month total for the special: about **$418.73**
- Straight 12-month CD: $10,000 × 4.10% = **$410.00**

Under those assumptions the special wins by roughly $8.73 on $10,000. Change the assumed parking rate and the answer can flip, which is the point: write the assumption down so you can see what the comparison depends on.

## Where to Look Rates Up, and How Often They Move

The institution's own rate sheet is the only binding source. Aggregator sites are useful for building a shortlist, but they lag, they sometimes show expired promotions, and they may omit the minimum balance required to earn the advertised APY. Confirm on the bank's site before you move money.

Two practical filters when you build that shortlist:

- **Credit unions:** check membership eligibility before you fall in love with a rate. Employer, geography, family relationship, or a small donation to an affiliated association are the common paths. Credit unions technically pay dividends on share certificates rather than interest on CDs; the disclosure still gives you an APY, and it is insured through the [NCUA](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage) rather than the FDIC.
- **Change frequency:** there is no schedule. A bank can reprice new CD offers any business day, and specials can end without notice. Checking daily is a waste of attention; setting a calendar reminder a week before a maturity date is not.

## Using the FDIC National Rate as Your Baseline

The FDIC publishes [national rates and national rate caps](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) monthly, broken out by product and term. Those caps have a regulatory function — they feed the rules limiting what institutions in weaker capital positions may pay — but for a saver they work as a free yardstick.

Use them like this: before comparing offers to each other, compare each one to the published average for the same term. Subtract, and express the gap in basis points (one basis point is 0.01 percentage points). An offer meaningfully above the average is doing something to earn your deposit and the "why" is usually visible in the fine print — a minimum balance tier, a relationship requirement, or a short promo window. An offer at or below the average is asking you to accept a below-market price for convenience, which is a legitimate choice as long as you make it knowingly.

Do the lookup yourself each time. The averages move, and a number quoted in any article, including this one, would be stale before you read it.

## The Same-Term, Same-Dollar Worksheet

Copy this into a note or spreadsheet and fill one column per offer. The last row is the only one that decides anything.

| Field | Where to find it | Offer A | Offer B |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deposit you will actually fund | Your own plan | | |
| Term, in days | Rate sheet / disclosure | | |
| APY | Disclosure (required, not optional) | | |
| Compounding and crediting frequency | Truth in Savings disclosure | | |
| Minimum to open vs minimum to earn the APY | Rate sheet footnotes | | |
| Early-withdrawal penalty formula | Account agreement | | |

Filling the "minimum to earn the APY" row separately from "minimum to open" catches the most common bait: an account you can open with a small deposit but which only pays the advertised yield at a much higher tier.

## Rate Traps That Survive a Careless Comparison

- **Teaser windows.** A high APY for an introductory number of days, then a drop to the standard tier for the remainder. Read the term the promo rate actually applies to, not the term of the CD.
- **Relationship requirements.** The advertised rate is available only if you also hold a checking account with a qualifying direct deposit. Price the cost of moving your payroll before you count the extra yield.
- **Tier cliffs.** The headline number applies to a balance band you are not in. Find the tier table and locate your own balance in it.
- **"Up to" language.** This usually signals the top of a tier structure or a step-up schedule. Ask what the first-day rate is.
- **New-money-only rules.** Funds already at the institution are excluded, so a transfer from your existing savings there does not qualify.
- **Unattractive default renewals.** A 13-month special that rolls into a standard 12-month product at whatever the bank posts that day. Put the maturity date in your calendar the day you open it — the grace period is short, and what to do inside it is covered in the piece on [cashing out a CD early](/SmartMoneyDaily/2026/05/25/what-happens-when-you-cash-out-a-cd-early/).

## How Penalties, Fees, and Tax Shrink the Winner's Margin

Rate differences between competitive offers are usually small. The costs attached to the account are not, so they decide more comparisons than the rate does.

**The penalty dominates if there is any chance you break the CD.** Suppose a $10,000 one-year CD at 4.00% APY carries a penalty of 180 days' simple interest at the nominal rate. That penalty is $10,000 × 0.04 × 180/365 ≈ $197. Now suppose you chose that CD over a competing offer because it paid 0.15 percentage points more — worth about $15 over the year on the same deposit. One early exit erases roughly thirteen years of that advantage. If your cash-need date is uncertain, the penalty schedule and the term length matter far more than the rate, which is the argument laid out in the comparison of [short-term versus long-term CDs](/SmartMoneyDaily/2026/06/08/should-you-opt-for-a-short-term-or-long-term-cd/).

**Fees are rarer on CDs than on other deposit accounts, but not absent.** Check the fee schedule for paper statement charges, wire fees on the outgoing maturity payment, and any account service fee that applies when the CD is your only relationship with the institution.

**Brokered CDs carry costs that are not called fees.** A markup can be embedded in the price, and selling before maturity means accepting whatever the secondary market pays that day, which may be less than what you put in.

**Tax lands on interest in the year it is credited**, even for a multi-year CD where you cannot touch the money yet. Your institution reports it on [Form 1099-INT](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-1099-int). For example, suppose a CD credits $1,000 of interest for the year and your combined federal and state marginal rate is 25%. You keep $750. That does not change which offer wins — tax applies to both — but it changes whether the difference is worth the paperwork of opening an account at a new institution.

## Rate Shopping Questions Worth Answering

### Do all banks change CD rates when the Fed moves?

No. Policy changes influence the direction of deposit pricing, but each institution decides how much to pass along and when. Some move within days, others take months, and some leave certain terms untouched entirely because they have no funding need there.

### Is a higher rate at an unfamiliar online bank riskier?

The rate itself is not a risk signal. What matters is whether the institution is federally insured and whether your balance sits within the coverage rules — verify the charter in the FDIC's BankFind tool or the NCUA's credit union lookup before funding, and confirm the exact legal entity name, since brands and charters do not always match.

### Should I chase a small APY difference?

Run the dollars first. On modest balances a fraction of a percentage point may be worth a few dollars a year, which rarely justifies opening and maintaining another account. On larger balances the same gap can be worth real money. The worksheet above turns the question into arithmetic instead of instinct.

### What happens if rates rise right after I lock?

Nothing happens to your existing CD — its rate is fixed for the term, which is the trade you accepted. Your options are to hold, to break it and pay the penalty if the new rate covers that cost, or to leave future maturities shorter so you can reprice sooner.

### Can I negotiate a CD rate?

Sometimes. Community banks and credit unions occasionally match a competitor's published rate on larger deposits. Ask before you fund the account, point to the specific published offer, and get any exception confirmed in writing on the disclosure.

## Before You Sign

Five steps, in order: shortlist from an aggregator, verify each rate on the institution's own site, look up the FDIC national rate for that term as a baseline, fill the worksheet with the same deposit and the same number of days, and read the penalty and renewal clauses before you look at the rate again. The offer that wins the last row of the worksheet is the offer — not the one with the largest font on the rate table.
