> **Short answer:** A money market fund is a mutual fund that holds short-term debt and tries to keep its share price at $1. It is regulated by the SEC, not insured by the FDIC, and its quoted yield already reflects fund expenses. It is a reasonable place for cash you will not need within 48 hours. It is a poor place for the money you would grab in a genuine emergency.

The single most expensive mistake with this product is a naming problem. A **money market account** is a deposit at a bank or credit union, covered by federal deposit insurance. A **money market fund** is an investment product bought through a brokerage. The names differ by one word; the protections differ completely. Everything below assumes you want the fund, and want to know exactly what you are buying.

## What a Money Market Fund Actually Holds

A money market fund pools money from many shareholders and buys short-term debt: Treasury bills, government agency paper, repurchase agreements, bank certificates of deposit, and — depending on the fund — corporate commercial paper. The holdings mature in days or months, not years, which is what keeps the share price steady.

Most of these funds are managed to hold a share price of $1.00. That is a target, not a promise. Under SEC Rule 2a-7, funds must limit the credit quality, maturity, and concentration of what they hold, and must keep minimum shares of the portfolio in assets that can be turned into cash daily and weekly. Those rules make the $1.00 target realistic. They do not make it guaranteed, and no fund prospectus claims otherwise.

Your return arrives as dividends, usually declared daily and paid monthly. The dividend rises and falls with short-term rates. When the [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy.htm) moves policy rates, the securities the fund buys next week reprice, and the fund's yield follows within weeks — faster than most bank deposit rates move, in both directions. There is no lock-in and no fixed rate to compare against.

## Government, Prime, and Municipal: Three Different Risk Profiles

"Money market fund" is a category, not a product. The three main varieties behave differently enough that picking the wrong one is the second most common error after confusing funds with accounts.

| Fund type | Mostly holds | Federal tax on distributions | State tax | Liquidity-fee exposure | Who it suits |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Government / Treasury | Treasury bills, government agency debt, repos backed by them | Taxable | The portion from direct Treasury obligations may be state-tax exempt, depending on your state's rules | Not subject to the mandatory liquidity fee applied to institutional prime and tax-exempt funds | Most savers parking cash; the conservative default |
| Prime | The above plus commercial paper, bank CDs, other short-term corporate debt | Taxable | Taxable | Institutional share classes carry a mandatory liquidity fee once net redemptions cross a set threshold | Savers accepting modest credit exposure for a slightly higher yield |
| Municipal / tax-exempt | Short-term municipal debt, often single-state | Usually exempt as exempt-interest dividends; some issues can trigger AMT | Frequently exempt in a single-state fund | Institutional tax-exempt classes fall under the same fee framework | High-bracket savers, after running taxable-equivalent math |

Two practical notes. First, the fee and gate rules that get discussed online apply to *institutional* share classes of prime and tax-exempt funds; retail government funds sit outside that framework. Check the share class you are actually buying, which is printed on the fact sheet, not the fund family's marketing page. Second, a municipal fund only wins if your tax bracket is high enough. The comparison is taxable-equivalent yield: divide the tax-exempt yield by one minus your marginal rate. Suppose a municipal fund yields 2.80% and your combined marginal rate is 32%. Then 2.80% ÷ (1 − 0.32) = 4.12%, and a taxable fund needs to beat 4.12% to win. Run that arithmetic with your own bracket before assuming the tax break helps.

## 7-Day Yield Minus Expense Ratio Is the Wrong Formula

This trips up careful people, so it is worth being exact.

The standardized **7-day yield** a fund reports is calculated *after* the fund's expenses come out. Subtracting the expense ratio from it, as plenty of comparison articles suggest, double-counts the cost and understates what you would earn. The expense ratio is already inside the number.

What you should check instead is whether that yield is being propped up:

- **Subsidized vs unsubsidized yield.** Many funds waive part of their fee. The fact sheet shows the yield with the waiver and, in a footnote, the yield without it. Waivers expire. The unsubsidized figure is the floor of what happens when it does.
- **Gross vs net expense ratio.** Same idea from the cost side. A wide gap between the two means the attractive number depends on a waiver the fund can end.
- **7-day yield vs 7-day effective yield.** The plain 7-day yield is a simple annualization. The *effective* version compounds. A bank's APY also compounds. So when you set a fund against a savings account, the effective yield is the honest comparison. Suppose a fund shows a 4.05% 7-day yield and a 4.13% 7-day effective yield while a savings account quotes 4.10% APY: the fund is ahead by a hair, not behind.

One more asymmetry. A fund's yield is backward-looking — it describes the last seven days. A bank APY is a forward quote the bank can change tomorrow. Neither is a promise about next month, but they are not the same kind of number, and a table that stacks them in one column without saying so is misleading you.

## Not FDIC-Insured: What SIPC Covers and What It Does Not

This is the section to read twice.

Money market funds are **not** covered by [FDIC deposit insurance](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance), and not by [NCUA share insurance](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage) either. Those programs cover deposits — checking, savings, money market *accounts*, and CDs — at insured banks and credit unions, up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured institution, per ownership category. A mutual fund share is not a deposit, so it is outside that system entirely. If you want the mechanics of what insurance actually covers, the [FDIC insurance guide](/SmartMoneyDaily/2026/05/29/what-is-fdic-insurance-and-how-does-it-protect-your-money/) walks through the covered and excluded product list.

What you get instead is SIPC protection, and it protects against a different failure. SIPC steps in when the *brokerage* holding your account fails and customer assets go missing. It works to restore your securities and cash positions. It does not insure the value of what you own. If the brokerage is fine and the fund itself loses money, SIPC pays nothing. That distinction — custodian failure versus investment loss — is the whole point.

So the honest summary: a bank deposit protects your principal by federal guarantee; a money market fund protects your principal through portfolio rules, diversification, and short maturities. The second is strong. It is not the same thing.

## Breaking the Buck, Liquidity Fees, and What Changed

"Breaking the buck" means the share price falls below $1.00. It is rare, and it has happened — most famously to a large prime fund during the September 2008 credit freeze, an event that triggered a rewrite of the rules governing these products.

The regulatory response has come in waves, and the current framework does three things worth knowing:

1. **Liquidity floors.** Funds must hold minimum percentages of the portfolio in assets convertible to cash within one day and within one week. The prospectus and the fund's daily disclosure page state the current levels and where the fund sits against them.
2. **Mandatory liquidity fees** on institutional prime and institutional tax-exempt funds when net redemptions in a day exceed a defined share of assets. The fee makes exiting savers bear the cost of the selling their exit forces, rather than pushing it onto everyone who stays.
3. **Redemption gates were removed** from the framework in favor of that fee mechanism. Gates could freeze redemptions outright; a fee prices the exit instead of blocking it.

None of that applies to a retail government fund in normal conditions. But "in normal conditions" is doing work in that sentence, and the reason to know the machinery is that a crisis is exactly when you would want your emergency cash and exactly when these clauses activate.

## Settlement Timing: Why Fund Shares Are Not Same-Day Cash

Redeeming fund shares is a trade, and trades have a schedule. The chain looks like this:

1. You place the redemption before the fund's cutoff time. Miss the cutoff and the order prices the next business day.
2. The redemption settles — often same day for government funds held at the sponsoring broker, often the next business day elsewhere.
3. Cash lands in your brokerage account.
4. You transfer it out by ACH to a bank, which adds business days of its own, or wire it for a fee.

Weekends and holidays stretch every step. Compare that to a money market account at your own bank, where a transfer between your accounts is usually instant and a debit card works at 2 a.m. Do not take a general claim about "next-day access" from any article, this one included — open your fund's prospectus, find the cutoff time and settlement language, and time one small test redemption before you rely on it.

## Money Market Fund vs Money Market Account, Line by Line

| | Money market fund | Money market account |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A mutual fund share, governed by SEC Rule 2a-7 | A deposit at a bank or credit union |
| Insurance | None from FDIC or NCUA; SIPC covers brokerage failure only | FDIC or NCUA insured up to $250,000 per depositor, per institution, per ownership category |
| Can principal fall | Yes, in principle | No, within insured limits |
| Yield quoted as | 7-day yield and 7-day effective yield, backward-looking, changes daily | APY, quoted forward, variable, often tiered by balance |
| Costs | Expense ratio, already inside the quoted yield, plus any brokerage account fees | Monthly maintenance, minimum balance, excess transaction and wire fees |
| Getting cash out | Redeem, wait for settlement, then transfer | Withdrawal, transfer, and frequently checks or a debit card |
| Tax form | Form 1099-DIV | Form 1099-INT |
| Where to verify it | Fund prospectus and fact sheet | The bank's Truth in Savings disclosure and fee schedule |

If the right column is what you actually want, start with [how a money market account works](/SmartMoneyDaily/2026/06/02/what-is-a-money-market-account-and-how-does-it-work/) rather than shopping funds.

## Taxes: The Form Is Different, and So Is the Treatment

Fund distributions are dividends, not interest, even though the underlying return comes from debt. That changes the paperwork and sometimes the tax bill.

- **Reporting.** Fund distributions arrive on Form 1099-DIV. Bank interest arrives on Form 1099-INT. If you hold both, expect both forms, and check that your own records match before filing. The [IRS guidance on investment income](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc403) covers how each is reported.
- **Ordinary income.** Taxable money market fund distributions are generally taxed at your ordinary income rate, not the preferential qualified-dividend rate. Do not assume the lower rate applies.
- **State treatment.** The share of a fund's income derived from direct U.S. Treasury obligations may be exempt from state income tax. Fund companies publish a percentage breakdown each January for exactly this purpose. If you live in a high-tax state, this can be worth real money, and it is why a [Treasury-focused fund or direct Treasury purchase](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/) sometimes beats a higher-headline prime fund after tax.
- **Municipal funds.** Distributions are typically exempt-interest dividends at the federal level, potentially exempt at the state level in a single-state fund, and possibly subject to AMT. Run the taxable-equivalent formula from earlier before you choose one.

## Is a Fund a Fair Home for an Emergency Fund?

An emergency account has to clear three tests: you can reach the money fast, the balance you counted on is the balance that is there, and getting it out costs nothing.

A money market fund passes the third test cleanly and the first two only partly. Settlement plus an ACH transfer is not same-day cash. And while the odds of principal loss are low, the scenario in which it is not low — a credit market seizure — is correlated with the scenario in which people lose jobs and need their emergency fund. That correlation is the argument against putting the whole thing in a fund.

The workable arrangement is tiered:

- **Tier 1, roughly one month of essential expenses.** Insured deposits at the bank you already use, reachable by card or instant transfer.
- **Tier 2, the next two to four months.** A high-yield savings account or money market account at an insured institution. Slower, still guaranteed.
- **Tier 3, anything beyond a full target.** A government money market fund is defensible here, where the money is a reserve rather than a first response.

Sizing that target is its own exercise — the [emergency fund sizing guide](/SmartMoneyDaily/2026/05/09/emergency-fund-basics-how-much-should-you-really-save/) works through essential-expense counting, and [where to keep an emergency fund](/SmartMoneyDaily/2026/07/20/how-to-effectively-use-high-yield-savings-for-emergency-funds/) compares access speeds across account types.

## Reading a Fund Fact Sheet in Five Minutes

Skip the performance chart. Find these ten items in order.

| What to find | Where it usually is | Why it decides something |
|---|---|---|
| Fund category: government, prime, or municipal | Fund name and investment objective | Sets your credit exposure and whether fee rules apply |
| Share class and its minimum investment | Share class table | Retail and institutional classes follow different rules and have different costs |
| 7-day yield and 7-day effective yield | Yield block near the top | The effective figure is what compares to a bank APY |
| Subsidized vs unsubsidized yield | Footnote beneath the yields | Shows how much of the headline depends on a waiver |
| Gross and net expense ratio | Fees and expenses table | A wide gap means the same waiver risk from the cost side |
| Weighted average maturity (WAM) | Portfolio characteristics | Shorter WAM means the yield tracks rate changes faster |
| Weighted average life (WAL) | Portfolio characteristics | Flags longer-dated floating-rate holdings a WAM figure hides |
| Daily and weekly liquid assets | Portfolio composition, or the fund's daily disclosure page | The fund's own cushion, updated daily |
| Liquidity fee language | Prospectus, redemption section | Tells you whether your share class is exposed at all |
| Cutoff time and settlement terms | Prospectus, or the broker's trading page | Determines whether "fast" means hours or days |

If a fund's page will not give you all ten within five minutes, that is information too.

## Where This Leaves You

A money market fund is a competent product doing an unglamorous job. It holds short-term debt, passes through prevailing short rates, and costs you an expense ratio already netted out of the quoted yield. Compare funds on effective yield, on the unsubsidized figure, and on category — not on the marketing number.

What it is not is a bank account. No federal deposit guarantee, no instant cash, and a set of crisis-era clauses that exist because the crisis case is real. Keep the money you might need this week in insured deposits where the guarantee is written into law, use a fund for reserves that can wait a few business days, and read your brokerage's own fee schedule while you are at it — account-level charges sit outside the expense ratio and can quietly reverse a yield advantage.

Next step: pull the fact sheet for the specific fund and share class you are considering, and run the ten-item scan above. If you cannot answer the insurance question and the settlement question from the fund's own documents, you are not ready to fund the account.
