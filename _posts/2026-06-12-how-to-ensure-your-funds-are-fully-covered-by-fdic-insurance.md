> **What to do:** Deposit insurance is capped at $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. To cover more, you add banks with separate charters, add ownership categories at one bank, hand the splitting to a deposit network, or move part of the balance into Treasuries. Anything above the ceiling is an unsecured claim against a failed bank, so the fix has to happen before there is a problem, not after.

This is the execution guide. If you are still working out what the cap covers and how the categories are counted, read [what FDIC insurance actually covers](/2026/05/29/what-is-fdic-insurance-and-how-does-it-protect-your-money/) and [how the $250,000 limit is calculated](/2026/06/01/what-limits-does-fdic-insurance-have-on-your-funds/) first. What follows assumes you already know the rule and now need a layout for the money.

## When You Cross the Line: Balances That Trigger a Rework

Most people do not decide to become underinsured. A balance drifts over the line while nobody is watching. These are the moments that put a household above the cap:

- A lump sum lands: home sale proceeds, an inheritance, a severance payout, a business distribution.
- Several accounts at one bank are held in the same single name — checking, high-yield savings, and a CD all count toward one $250,000 bucket, not three.
- A CD matures and rolls over with accumulated interest included in the new principal.
- Two accounts you think are at "different banks" turn out to share one charter after a merger.
- A fintech app holds your cash at a partner bank where you already have your own account, so the two balances stack.

The trigger is not a feeling that you have a lot of money. It is a single number: total deposits at one insured institution within one ownership category. Run that number per bank, not across your whole net worth.

## Route 1: Spread Across Unrelated Charters

The simplest route, and the one that fails most often for a reason nobody expects: the unit of coverage is the charter, not the brand name. Two consumer brands, two websites, two apps, and two logos can sit on top of one FDIC certificate. In that case your balances are added together against a single $250,000 limit.

Verify before you fund anything. Look each institution up in [FDIC BankFind](https://banks.data.fdic.gov/bankfind-suite/bankfind) and write down the FDIC certificate number, not the brand. Two different cert numbers mean two separate limits. The same cert number means one limit, no matter how different the marketing looks.

Mergers deserve their own note. When an insured bank is absorbed by another, the FDIC's deposit insurance rules give the assumed deposits a temporary separate insurance period rather than merging the limits overnight, and CDs from the acquired institution get treated on their own maturity schedule. The transition period is finite. Read the current text on the [FDIC's deposit insurance pages](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/) when a merger notice arrives, put the end date on a calendar, and move the excess before it expires.

Credit unions work the same way through a different agency. Share accounts at a federally insured credit union are covered by the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund at the same $250,000 standard, administered by the [NCUA](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage). A bank charter and a credit union charter are separate institutions, so a household can use both. What you cannot do is assume one covers the other.

The real cost of this route is administrative: more logins, more statements, more tax forms in January, and transfer timing between institutions that runs on ACH rather than instantly.

## Route 2: Use Separate Ownership Categories at One Bank

You can also expand coverage without leaving your bank, because the limit applies separately to each ownership category. Deposit insurance categories are defined in the FDIC's regulations at 12 CFR Part 330, and the practical version looks like this:

| Ownership category | How the limit is applied | Common use |
|---|---|---|
| Single account | $250,000 total for all single-name deposits at that bank | Everyday checking, savings, CDs in one name |
| Joint account | $250,000 per co-owner for their share across all joint accounts at that bank | Two equal owners produce $500,000 of coverage |
| Certain retirement accounts | $250,000 total for IRA-type deposits at that bank | Bank-held IRA CDs and IRA savings |
| Trust accounts (revocable and irrevocable combined) | $250,000 per eligible beneficiary, capped at five beneficiaries per owner, per bank | Living trusts, payable-on-death designations |
| Employee benefit plan | Separate category with its own rules | Small-business plan deposits |
| Business accounts (corporation, partnership, association) | $250,000 for the entity, separate from the owners' personal accounts | Operating cash for an LLC or corporation |

Two points here save people from bad math. First, the trust category has a ceiling. Under the FDIC's revised trust rule, revocable and irrevocable trust deposits are combined and coverage stops at five eligible beneficiaries per owner, per bank — $1,250,000 maximum, not an unlimited amount that grows with every name you add. Older articles that promise coverage per beneficiary with no cap are describing rules that no longer apply. Second, adding a category only works if the bank's records show it. A joint account needs both owners on the signature record with equal rights; a payable-on-death designation needs the beneficiaries named in the account records. The title on the account is what gets read at resolution, not what you intended.

## Route 3: Network Deposit Services and Sweep Programs

Some banks offer a service that takes a large deposit, splits it across a network of other insured institutions in sub-$250,000 pieces, and gives you one statement and one relationship. Reciprocal deposit networks and brokered deposit sweep programs both work on this idea. It is genuinely convenient for a seven-figure cash balance, and it is where the sloppiest disclosures live. Ask five questions before signing:

1. Can I get the current list of destination institutions, and am I already a customer of any of them? Overlap silently rebuilds the problem you were solving.
2. Is my money placed in **deposit accounts** at insured banks, or swept into a **money market fund**? Only the first is FDIC-insured.
3. What does the statement show — a single lump, or the allocation per bank with each institution named?
4. What does the service cost, in fees or in a lower rate than the same bank pays on a direct account?
5. Are the records held in a way that identifies me as the owner of each slice?

Question five is the one that matters for fintech apps. An app is usually not a bank. It holds pooled funds at one or more partner banks, and pass-through coverage depends on the partner being insured, the account being properly titled for the benefit of customers, and the app's ledger accurately showing your share. If the ledger is wrong, the coverage claim gets slow, and the app itself failing is not an insured event at all. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/) has published consumer guidance on this distinction; before you park a six-figure balance in an app, find the partner bank's name in the deposit agreement and confirm the cert number yourself.

## Route 4: Treasuries and Government Funds, a Different Kind of Protection

Above roughly a million dollars, splitting deposits across charters becomes a chore. The alternative is to stop relying on deposit insurance for part of the balance.

Treasury bills, notes, and bonds are direct obligations of the U.S. government. They are not FDIC-insured and do not need to be — there is no per-account cap, because the protection comes from the issuer rather than an insurance fund. You can buy them without a broker at [TreasuryDirect](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/). The trade-offs are real: your money is committed until maturity unless you sell in the secondary market at whatever price it fetches that day, and state income tax treatment of Treasury interest differs from bank interest.

Government money market funds are the other common landing spot, and they are a different animal entirely. A fund is a security, not a deposit. It is not FDIC-insured, its value can move, and brokerage account protection covers custody failure rather than investment loss. If that is where a chunk of your cash is headed, read [how money market funds work and where their risk hides](/2026/06/25/a-comprehensive-guide-to-finding-the-right-money-market-fund/) before you treat the balance as equivalent to a savings account.

| Route | Coverage source | Effort to set up | Ongoing maintenance | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Separate charters | FDIC/NCUA, per institution | Moderate — one application each | Rate checks, transfers, extra 1099s | Shared charters, mergers |
| Ownership categories | FDIC, per category | Low — retitling at one bank | Confirm titling after any life change | Five-beneficiary trust cap |
| Deposit network | FDIC, via destination banks | Low — one agreement | Review the allocation list | Overlap, sweeps into funds, fees |
| Treasuries / government funds | U.S. Treasury / none | Moderate — new account | Maturity or reinvestment decisions | Not insured; funds can lose value |

## Interest Pushes You Over: Rebalancing Before Maturity

A CD funded exactly at the cap is engineered to become uninsured. Interest accrues on top of principal, and the FDIC limit applies to principal plus accrued interest.

Work an example with assumed numbers. Suppose you place $245,000 in a 24-month CD and suppose it pays 4.00% APY. After one year the balance is about $254,800, and it crosses $250,000 somewhere around the six-month mark: $250,000 ÷ $245,000 = 1.0204, and at 4.00% that multiple arrives at roughly 0.52 years. By maturity the balance is near $264,992. That means about $15,000 sits uninsured for the back half of the term at a bank where you thought you were fine.

Two clean fixes. Either fund the CD at a principal that still lands under the cap at maturity — divide your target ceiling by the growth factor for the full term — or elect to have interest paid out monthly into an account at a different institution instead of compounding inside the CD.

To check your own numbers, enter your opening balance, the APY on your disclosure, the compounding frequency, and the term below, then compare the ending balance to $250,000.

## A Quarterly Coverage Audit You Can Run in 15 Minutes

Do this four times a year and after any large deposit, marriage, divorce, death in the family, or bank merger notice.

1. List every deposit account: institution, FDIC or NCUA certificate number, ownership category, current balance.
2. Group the list by certificate number, then by category within each certificate. Add up each group.
3. Flag any group above $250,000 — or above the applicable trust or joint total.
4. Project forward one year. Add expected interest and any scheduled deposits, then flag groups that will cross the line before your next audit.
5. Run the official estimator at [EDIE, the FDIC's Electronic Deposit Insurance Estimator](https://edie.fdic.gov/), which applies the current category rules to the accounts you enter.
6. Fix the flagged groups using one of the four routes, and confirm the titling change appears on your next statement.

Step six is the one people skip. A retitling request that was never processed reads, at resolution, exactly like a request that was never made.

## Documents a Failure Resolution Team Will Ask For

If an institution fails, the FDIC determines coverage from the bank's own deposit account records. Your paperwork matters when those records are ambiguous — with trusts, beneficiary designations, and fiduciary arrangements especially. Keep these where you can reach them without the failed bank's website:

- Recent statements for every account, showing balances and account titles.
- The trust instrument and the current list of eligible beneficiaries.
- Payable-on-death or in-trust-for designation forms.
- Retirement plan documents for IRA-type deposits.
- For fintech balances: the deposit agreement naming the partner bank, plus statements showing your individual sub-ledger balance.

One thing to be clear-eyed about: money above the insured limit does not vanish, but it is not guaranteed either. Uninsured depositors receive a receivership claim and are paid from the proceeds of the failed bank's assets, in an amount and on a timeline that depend on what those assets fetch. That is a recovery process, not insurance, which is exactly why the restructuring belongs on this side of the event.

## Which Route Fits Which Balance

| Balance in cash | Practical starting layout | Why |
|---|---|---|
| $250,000 – $500,000 | One extra ownership category or one second bank | A joint account or a second charter closes the gap with almost no ongoing work |
| $500,000 – $1,000,000 | Two or three charters plus categories, reviewed quarterly | Enough moving parts to need an audit, still manageable by hand |
| Above $1,000,000 | Deposit network for the working balance, Treasuries or government funds for the rest | Hand-splitting across a dozen banks costs more attention than it returns |

Pick the route by how much maintenance you will genuinely do, not by which one has the highest theoretical ceiling. A layout you audit twice a year beats an elegant structure you set up once and never verify.

## Coverage Gaps: Questions Worth Checking Twice

**Does opening five accounts at one bank give me five limits?** No. Balances in the same ownership category at the same bank are added together, then compared to one $250,000 limit. Only a different category or a different charter creates a new limit.

**Is a brokered CD insured?** The underlying deposit is insured at the issuing bank, subject to the same per-depositor, per-bank cap — which means a brokered CD from a bank where you already hold deposits stacks with those balances. Ask which institution issues each CD before buying.

**My balance is over the cap for two weeks while a house closes. Do I need to restructure?** Short exposure is a judgment call, but it is a real exposure, and a wire to a second institution usually takes less time than reading this section. If the amount would materially hurt to lose, split it.
