> **Short answer:** Breaking a CD before maturity costs a fixed number of days of interest, written into your account agreement as something like "90 days' interest" or "365 days' interest." It is not a percentage of your balance and it is not a flat fee. If the penalty is larger than the interest you have earned so far, the difference comes out of your principal, and that is legal.

Most people find out the number too late. The penalty is disclosed once, at account opening, in the paperwork nobody keeps. This page shows you where to find it, how to turn it into a dollar figure, and how to decide whether breaking the CD is a loss or a win.

## The Penalty Is Days of Interest, Not a Percentage

Banks and credit unions almost always express the early withdrawal penalty as a number of days or months of interest on the amount withdrawn. Read that literally: it is calculated on the withdrawn amount at the CD's own rate, regardless of how long you have actually held the money.

That structure produces three consequences people do not expect:

- **The penalty does not shrink as you approach maturity.** A 365-day penalty costs the same in month 3 and month 55 of a five-year term.
- **It is charged at your CD's rate, not the current market rate.** A low-rate CD carries a proportionally smaller dollar penalty.
- **It applies to the amount withdrawn.** Some institutions allow partial withdrawals; the penalty then applies only to that slice, though many contracts require you to close the whole account.

There is a federal floor underneath this. Regulation D's definition of a time deposit (12 CFR 204.2(c)(1)(i)) requires a penalty of at least seven days' simple interest on funds withdrawn within the first six days after deposit. Banks may set penalties far above that floor, and nothing in federal law caps them.

What federal law does require is disclosure. Truth in Savings, implemented as Regulation DD ([12 CFR Part 1030](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/)), requires a time account's disclosure to state the amount or method of calculating any early withdrawal penalty and whether it can reach your principal. That paperwork is the only authoritative source for your number; a phone rep's summary is not.

## Decoding the Clause in Your Disclosure

Penalty language is short and easy to misread. Here is what the common wordings actually mean and where each detail lives.

| What the disclosure says | What it means in practice | Where to confirm it |
|---|---|---|
| "90 days' interest on the amount withdrawn" | Simple interest at your CD rate on the withdrawn dollars for 90 days, deducted at closing | Truth in Savings disclosure, "Early Withdrawal Penalty" section |
| "365 days' interest, or all interest earned, whichever is greater" | The stated day count is a floor, not a ceiling; a long-held CD can lose more | Same section; look for "whichever is greater" |
| "The penalty may reduce principal" | Confirms the bank will dip into your deposit if earned interest is not enough | Required Reg DD statement; usually one sentence |
| "Penalty applies to withdrawals in excess of accrued interest" | You may withdraw earned interest freely; the penalty starts past that | Account agreement, withdrawal terms |
| "Bank may, at its discretion, refuse an early withdrawal request" | The bank can make you wait until maturity, penalty or not | Account agreement, general terms |
| "No penalty after the first six days" (no-penalty CD) | Withdrawal is free after the federal minimum holding window, but usually all-or-nothing | Product disclosure for that specific CD |
| "Interest is credited at maturity" | You may have no credited interest to absorb a penalty, so principal takes the whole hit | Disclosure, "Compounding and Crediting" |

Two lines cause most of the surprises: "whichever is greater," which makes the headline day count meaningless late in a long term, and the discretion clause, which makes an early withdrawal a request rather than a right. Both belong on your checklist alongside the rate; our guide to [the terms to check before signing a CD](/2026/07/08/how-to-choose-the-best-certificate-of-deposit-for-your-savings-goals/) covers the rest.

## When the Penalty Eats Principal

This is the part savers refuse to believe until it happens. If you close a CD before it has earned enough interest to cover the penalty, the bank takes the shortfall out of your deposit. You get back less than you put in.

Work through it. Suppose you put $10,000 into a CD paying 3.00%, with a disclosed penalty of 180 days' interest, and you close it after 60 days.

- Interest earned: $10,000 × 3.00% × (60 ÷ 365) = **$49.32**
- Penalty owed: $10,000 × 3.00% × (180 ÷ 365) = **$147.95**
- Shortfall taken from principal: $147.95 − $49.32 = **$98.63**
- Cash returned: **$9,901.37**

Reg DD permits this as long as the disclosure warned you, which is why that sentence about principal is mandatory. Exposure is worst in the first months of a long term, and worst of all on a CD that credits interest only at maturity: nothing has been credited to absorb the hit.

## Break-Even Math: Sometimes Breaking It Wins

A penalty is not automatically a reason to stay. If rates have moved up meaningfully since you locked in, paying a penalty to move into a higher-yielding account can leave you ahead. The question is arithmetic, not nerve.

**The comparison:** penalty cost today versus extra interest earned over the days remaining.

Example A — staying wins. Assume a $10,000 CD paying 4.00% with a 365-day penalty, 30 months left, and an alternative paying 4.75%.

- Penalty: $10,000 × 4.00% = **$400**
- Extra yield: 0.75 percentage points × $10,000 × 2.5 years ≈ **$188**
- Net: −$212. Leave it alone.

Example B — breaking wins. Assume a $10,000 CD paying 3.00% with a 180-day penalty, 36 months left, and an alternative paying 4.50%.

- Penalty: $10,000 × 3.00% × (180 ÷ 365) ≈ **$148**
- Extra yield: 1.50 percentage points × $10,000 × 3 years ≈ **$450**
- Net: +$302. Breaking it is the better trade, assuming the new rate holds.

That last assumption matters. If the replacement is a variable-rate savings account, the gap can close at any time; if it is another fixed CD, the gap is contractual. Both examples ignore compounding and taxes, which is fine for a decision this coarse.

Here is the same logic as a threshold. Assuming a CD paying 4.00%, this is roughly how much higher the replacement rate has to be, in percentage points, before breaking pays for itself.

| Penalty size | 1 year left | 2 years left | 3 years left | 4 years left |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 90 days' interest | +0.99 pp | +0.49 pp | +0.33 pp | +0.25 pp |
| 180 days' interest | +1.97 pp | +0.99 pp | +0.66 pp | +0.49 pp |
| 270 days' interest | +2.96 pp | +1.48 pp | +0.99 pp | +0.74 pp |
| 365 days' interest | +4.00 pp | +2.00 pp | +1.33 pp | +1.00 pp |

The pattern is the useful part: the more time left, the easier it is to justify breaking, because a one-time cost is spread over a longer earning period. A CD in its final year almost never justifies an early exit.

## Exceptions That Waive the Penalty

Waivers exist, and they are narrower than the internet suggests.

**Death or legal incompetence.** Regulation D's time deposit rules exempt withdrawals made after the death of an owner or after an owner is adjudicated legally incompetent. Most institutions honor this, though the estate or conservator still has to produce documentation.

**IRA CDs at required distribution age.** Many banks waive their own penalty on withdrawals taken to satisfy a required minimum distribution. This is separate from the tax side: distributions from an IRA before age 59½ can carry an additional 10% tax under [IRS rules on early distributions](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc557), and a bank waiver does nothing about that. Two different penalties, two different authorities.

**A failed bank that reprices your CD.** If your institution fails and an acquiring bank changes the rate on your transferred CD, you can generally close it without the early withdrawal penalty. Confirm the specifics with the resolution notice and the [FDIC's deposit insurance pages](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/), or with [NCUA](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage) if it was a credit union share certificate.

**Bank discretion.** Some institutions will waive or reduce a penalty for documented hardship. This is a policy, not a right, and it is not something to count on when choosing a term.

Notice what is missing from that list: job loss, medical bills, and a first home purchase are not general penalty exemptions on bank CDs, whatever the rules may be for retirement accounts. Assume no waiver unless your own disclosure names one.

## No-Penalty CDs and the Price of the Exit

A no-penalty CD, sometimes marketed as a liquid CD, lets you close the account after the federal minimum holding window without a penalty. The trade-offs are consistent enough to plan around:

- The rate is typically set below a comparable traditional CD, because the bank cannot count on the money staying.
- Withdrawal is usually all-or-nothing. You close the account and take the full balance; partial withdrawals are often not permitted.
- Terms cluster at the short end, so a no-penalty CD is rarely a substitute for a multi-year lock.

The honest comparison is not "no-penalty CD versus traditional CD." It is "no-penalty CD versus a [high-yield savings account](/2026/06/15/what-to-consider-when-opening-a-high-yield-savings-account/)." The CD's advantage is a rate fixed for the term; the savings account's advantage is unlimited partial access. If you might need part of the money, the savings account usually wins on structure alone.

## Alternatives to Breaking the CD

Before you close anything, check whether the cash can come from somewhere cheaper.

1. **Borrow against the CD.** Many banks lend against a pledged CD at a spread over its own rate. For a short stretch, interest on a small secured loan can cost less than a year of forfeited interest.
2. **Withdraw accrued interest only.** Where the disclosure permits interest withdrawals, taking earned interest usually carries no penalty. It is a small amount, but it is free.
3. **Break the smallest piece.** If you hold several CDs, close the one with the shortest remaining term and the smallest penalty rather than the largest balance.
4. **Take a partial withdrawal.** Where allowed, the penalty applies only to the amount withdrawn. Ask before you close the whole account.
5. **Wait for a maturity already coming.** If a rung matures in six weeks, bridging six weeks is cheaper than any penalty. That is the practical argument for [building a CD ladder](/2026/07/09/what-you-need-to-know-about-laddering-certificates-of-deposit/) instead of one lump-sum term.

## The Situations That Force People to Break a CD

Almost every early withdrawal traces back to a decision made at opening, not to bad luck.

- **Locking money that was really the emergency fund.** A CD is not an emergency account. Fund the cash reserve first, then term-deposit what is left over.
- **Choosing the term by rate instead of by date.** The right term is the one that matures when you need the money. Chasing an extra fraction of a point into a term you cannot hold is how penalties happen.
- **Missing the grace period and getting auto-renewed.** A renewed CD is a new term with a new maturity date. Money you meant to take out is locked again, and taking it out now costs a penalty.
- **Not knowing the penalty was days-of-interest based.** People who assume a small flat fee behave differently from people who know a year of interest is at stake.
- **Ignoring taxes on interest already credited.** Interest is taxable in the year it is credited even if you later forfeit some of it to a penalty, so a mid-term break can produce a tax bill on money you did not keep.

The fix for all five is the same: write the maturity date, the grace period, and the penalty formula into a calendar the day you open the account. If you want a fuller walkthrough of the product itself, start with [how a CD works from funding day to renewal](/2026/07/16/what-is-a-certificate-of-deposit-and-how-does-it-benefit-you/).

## Use the Grace Period Instead

The cheapest exit is the one that costs nothing: the grace period after maturity, commonly around ten days, during which you can withdraw, add funds, or change the term without a penalty. Miss it and the CD renews at whatever rate the bank posts that day, and the penalty clock starts over on a new term.

A short routine handles it:

1. Note the maturity date and the grace window length when you open the account.
2. Set two reminders, one two weeks before maturity and one on the maturity date.
3. During the window, compare the renewal rate against what else is available for the same term.
4. Decide explicitly: withdraw, renew, or re-term. Doing nothing is a decision, and it is usually the worst one.

## Before You Break the CD: Five Things People Ask

**Can the bank refuse to let me withdraw early?**
Yes. Most account agreements say early withdrawal is permitted at the institution's discretion. In practice banks approve these requests, but the contract does not obligate them to.

**Does the penalty apply to my principal or just my interest?**
It is calculated on the amount withdrawn, then deducted. If your earned interest does not cover it, the remainder comes out of principal, and your disclosure is required to tell you so.

**Is a penalty on a brokered CD the same thing?**
No. Brokered CDs generally have no early withdrawal penalty because you do not redeem them with the bank; you sell them on a secondary market at whatever a buyer will pay. Your result depends on rates and demand that day, which can be worse than a stated penalty or better.

**Do I still owe tax on interest I forfeited?**
Interest credited to you is reportable for that year. The penalty is reported separately in Box 2 of Form 1099-INT and claimed as an adjustment to income, which offsets the effect.

**How do I find my penalty if I lost the paperwork?**
Ask for the Truth in Savings disclosure for your specific account, in writing. Online banks usually keep it in the account documents section. Do not rely on a verbal quote for a number that may cost you hundreds of dollars.

## Run Your Own Numbers Before You Decide

Pull up your CD's disclosure and write down four numbers: the rate, the penalty in days, the maturity date, and the grace period. Run those against a realistic alternative rate. If the gap does not clear the threshold above, hold to maturity and set a reminder for the grace window. If it does clear, get the penalty confirmed in writing before you authorize the closure.
